Track 1 · Government Policy Pitch · JMSC6140

If a broadcaster uses AI to make what you see, it should tell you.

Show the Machine is a new AI chapter for Hong Kong's TV and Radio Codes of Practice. It has four rules: label AI content, keep synthetic footage out of news, name a human editor, and publish your AI policy. The Communications Authority can do this without a new law, through the review of the Codes it has already promised.

The problem

Hong Kong stations already air AI. No rule says when to tell viewers.

Television is still Hong Kong's main news source, and AI is now in drama, factual shows, weather and promos. But the Codes of Practice say nothing about AI. Nothing requires a label, a responsible editor or a line between illustration and real footage.

"Invisibility is by design."Sarah T. Roberts, Behind the Screen (2019), on hidden labour behind the platforms. AI production is hidden in the same way.
  1. 01

    The rule does not exist

    The Consumer Council told the Communications Authority (CA) that AI guidelines for local TV broadcasting "are yet to be in place". It asked for AI disclosure duties in the licence renewal.

    Consumer Council submission, 2025
  2. 02

    The public asked for labels

    The CA's own consultation report records public views that stations "should provide a clear note at a conspicuous position on the screen" for AI content. It names programmes that "had made extensive use of AI-generated footage and voice-overs".

    CA Public Report on free TV renewal, App. II paras 11.16, 12.10
  3. 03

    The renewal missed it

    TVB, ViuTV (HKTVE) and HOY got 12-year renewals on 13 October 2026. The new conditions cover local production, national education, subtitling and simulcasting. None of them covers AI.

    CEDB press release · RTHK
  4. 04

    The door is open now

    The CA says it "is minded to" review the Codes of Practice after the renewal, in a separate consultation. That review is our vehicle.

    CA Public Report, Ch. 4 para 3
  5. 05

    AI errors are already reaching audiences

    On 9 November 2026, RTHK removed the trailer for its AI short video on Sun Yat-sen. An outside educator had pointed out historical errors, including the number of subjects in which Sun received honours. A human check before release would have caught them.

    unwire.hk, 10 Nov 2026
  6. 06

    Fakes borrow the look of TV news

    Scammers have used deepfake "TVB news reports" to sell fake investments, with fake versions of officials and even news anchors. A trusted broadcast look is now something worth stealing.

    TVB News · SCMP
  7. 07

    Neighbours have acted

    The EU AI Act's deepfake transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026. Korea's AI Basic Act requires deepfake labels. On 17 September 2026, Mainland China's NRTA said programmes made or aired with AI must carry labels and pass human review.

    HK Law Society · MSIT Korea · Lianhe Zaobao

The proposal

One new chapter. Four rules. Tiered by genre.

The rules are strictest for news and current affairs and lighter for drama and entertainment. They apply to every TV and radio licensee and to RTHK. They regulate how content is made and disclosed. They do not regulate what a programme says. They cover both AI-enhanced work (AI helps a person) and AI-powered work (AI makes the content itself), because everything a broadcaster airs is audience-facing.

1
All genres, tiered

Tell the viewer

A clear label whenever AI generates or materially alters images, video, voice or a presenter. "Materially" means it changes what a reasonable viewer would believe happened, who spoke or what they said.

Routine tools such as colour correction, noise removal and checked transcription need no label.

2
News and current affairs

No synthetic reality in news

AI footage must never be presented as real footage of a news event. AI illustrations are allowed only with an on-screen label. No synthetic face or voice of a real person without that person's consent and a label. AI presenters must be identified as AI.

This is the core public-interest line. News is where viewers' right to know matters most.

3
News, current affairs, factual

A human signs off

Every AI-assisted news or factual item has a named responsible editor, recorded in a sign-off log kept for 12 months. AI subtitles and translations are checked by a person before broadcast.

This follows Wishart: never publish AI content without human oversight. The RTHK Sun Yat-sen case shows why.

4
All licensees and RTHK

Publish your AI policy

Each station publishes its AI-use policy and files a short annual AI transparency report with the CA. The report lists which programme types used AI, the main tools and vendors, and any AI-related corrections.

This answers "who depends on whom?" Viewers learn whose models sit behind their news.

+
Technical layer · news first · phased

Make the label travel: Content Credentials

Labels on screen disappear once a clip is cut and reposted. So in Phase 1b, stations pilot C2PA Content Credentials, a signed open standard, on the news clips they post online. Viewers and platforms can then check that a "TVB news" clip really came from TVB. This is the best defence against fake news reports used in scams. France Télévisions already signs its daily news programmes (IPTC). Fuji TV has used C2PA on air since June 2025, and Ukraine's public broadcaster Suspilne built its system with a team of three in about six months (IPTC Tokyo summit report).

The pilot is voluntary from 2028. The CA reviews in 2030 whether to make it a requirement for news.

How the label changes by genre

Same rule, different weight

Pick a genre and see the label it needs. News carries the strictest label. Drama only needs a notice before the programme and in the credits.

Draft text: new Chapter on Artificial Intelligence (excerpt)

  1. A licensee shall clearly inform the audience when artificial intelligence has been used to generate or materially alter any image, video, voice or presenter in a programme.
  2. In news and current affairs programmes, the information in para 1 shall be given on screen, for as long as the material is shown, in a form that is easy to notice and understand.
  3. A licensee shall not present material generated by artificial intelligence as actual footage or recording of a news event.
  4. A licensee shall not broadcast a synthetic image or voice of an identifiable real person without that person's consent and a clear label, except where the material itself is the subject of a news report and is labelled as such.
  5. For each news, current affairs or factual item made with the help of artificial intelligence, a licensee shall name a responsible editor and keep a record for 12 months, available to the Authority on request.
  6. A licensee shall publish its policy on the use of artificial intelligence and shall submit to the Authority an annual report in the form the Authority specifies.
Why this wording works in Hong Kong

It uses the same style as the existing Generic Code of Practice on Television Programme Standards, which the CA issues under section 3 of the Broadcasting Ordinance (Cap. 562). The Radio Code is issued under section 19 of Cap. 391 (CA). Each test is factual: was AI used, was it labelled, is an editor named? None asks the CA to judge whether a view is acceptable. The CA's report says it respects editorial independence (Ch. 4 para 4), and these rules keep it that way.

Who enforces

A points ladder, not a cliff edge

The CA enforces the chapter with the sanctions it already has under the Broadcasting Ordinance. Our addition is a published points guide, like driving-offence points. Stations can then see exactly how repeated breaches escalate.

Why we would not pull a licence first

If all three free TV stations refused at once, revoking their licences would punish viewers, not the stations. People would lose free, licensed news, and that is their right to know. So the penalties target the company's money and reputation. The public keeps its channel. Revocation stays where it is in the law today: a last resort for the most serious cases.

  1. Step 1

    Advice or strong advice

    A first minor breach, such as a label that is too small or too short.

    1 point
  2. Step 2

    Warning or serious warning

    A repeat breach within 24 months, or a missing label in news.

    2–3 points
  3. Step 3

    On-air correction or apology

    Synthetic footage passed off as real in news. Viewers are told the truth on the same channel.

    4 points
  4. Step 4

    Financial penalty

    Up to HK$200,000 the first time, HK$400,000 the second and HK$1,000,000 after that. These are the existing caps in s.28(3).

    6+ points
  5. Step 5

    Suspension or revocation

    Existing powers only: suspension of up to 30 days in the cases set out in the law; revocation by the Chief Executive in Council. A last resort. No new power is created.

    existing law

Sanction types: CA, Sanctions & Appeals. Penalty caps and suspension: Broadcasting Ordinance, Cap. 562, ss. 28 and 31. Precedent: in 2016 the CA moved to suspend ATV's licence for 30 days after repeated failures (HKSAR Government). The points values are our proposal for a published CA guideline.

Who pays

Three ways to check compliance. We pick the middle one.

Stations pay their own compliance costs: labels, a sign-off log, a policy page and an annual report. The question is how much checking the government pays for.

Detection does not depend only on the regulator. The RTHK Sun Yat-sen errors were spotted by an outside educator within days (unwire). Rule 4 reports give the public something to check against.

Scope

Why start with licensed broadcasters

Where the law already reaches

Licensed stations use public resources and accept licence conditions in return. The CA can add this chapter today. Newspapers, online outlets and self-media are not licensed in this way and are hard to define, so a binding rule for them would need new law. That is slower and riskier for press freedom.

Where trust is highest

65% of Hong Kong people rely on TV for news, and electronic media such as NowTV News, i-Cable News and RTHK are among the most trusted brands (Reuters Institute). A mistake by a trusted licensed station damages the whole system.

Phase 2: a template for everyone else

The 2026 Policy Address asks the Digital Policy Office to drive AI guidelines by sector (Policy Address). Online outlets such as HK01 and am730, which already use AI voices and video (Reuters Institute), can adopt the same chapter voluntarily.

In step with the region

Our rules match the direction of the EU (Art. 50), Korea (AI Basic Act) and the Mainland's NRTA, which pairs AI promotion with labels and human review. We are not asking Hong Kong to go against the trend. We are asking it to catch up.

Hard questions

The questions we have been asked, and our answers

The first two came from our peer stress test on 10 November. The others are the questions a policymaker panel will ask.

Stress test, 10 NovTV is losing viewers and money. Why regulate AI instead of helping stations use it?

We do both. The government already promotes AI in broadcasting: the Policy Address asks RTHK to use more AI, and TVB says AI could cut long-form production costs by 20–30% (Sina). Clear rules make that safe. A station that labels and signs off knows it is compliant. A station that hides AI and gets caught loses credibility, and because it is a licensed station, the whole licensed system loses credibility with it. The Mainland regulator takes the same view: encourage innovation and hold the line on labels and human review (Lianhe Zaobao).

Stress test, 10 NovIf all three TV stations ignore the rule, do you take their licences away?

No, not first. We use the points ladder: advice, then warnings, then an on-air correction, then fines of up to HK$1 million. Revocation stays a last resort under existing law. As lawmakers we have to plan for the worst case, and the worst case is the public losing its free, licensed news. That would harm their right to know more than any unlabelled clip.

Panel questionWho pays?

Stations pay their own compliance costs. These are low: a label template, a sign-off log, a policy page and one report a year. The CA pays for checking within its existing complaints and monitoring work, using Model B. No new office and no new law are needed.

Panel questionHow does the CA catch unlabelled AI?

Three ways. Public complaints through the existing CA system. Random spot checks of sign-off logs against what aired. And the stations' own annual reports, which create a public record that journalists and researchers can check. We do not rely on AI detectors, because they are unreliable. We rely on records and accountability.

Panel questionWhat stops a station from complying only on paper?

The paper has to match the screen. A spot check compares the sign-off log with what actually aired, so a log with no named editor, or an AI clip with no log entry, is a breach in itself. The annual report is public, so viewers, journalists and researchers can compare it with what they see. A false report moves a station up the points ladder faster than a missing label. This moves the claim "we use AI responsibly" up the Ladder of Verification, from what the company says to what others can check.

Panel questionIsn't giving a government regulator more power over newsrooms a press-freedom risk?

It is a fair concern in Hong Kong, so we designed for it. Every test is about method, not message: was AI used, was it labelled, is an editor named? The CA never judges whether a story is acceptable, and there is no pre-approval. The CA's own report says it respects editorial independence. The chapter also protects newsrooms: signed content helps prove that a fake "TVB report" is fake.

Panel questionWhy not newspapers and online media?

They are not licensed, so there is no legal hook, and "online media" is hard to define. We start where the law already reaches and where trust is highest. Phase 2 offers the same chapter as a voluntary standard for online outlets through the Digital Policy Office's sector guidelines.

Panel questionWill labels on everything make viewers ignore them, or make stations stop using AI?

That is why the rules are tiered. Routine edits need no label, and drama only needs a notice at the start and in the credits. Strict on-screen labels are only for news and factual recreations. We would test viewer understanding after 12 months and simplify to a single standard icon if needed.

Panel questionWhat if you are wrong?

We built in ways to notice and fix it. If labels confuse viewers, a 12-month audience survey shows it and we simplify. If stations under-label, complaints and spot checks show it and the ladder escalates. If the burden is too heavy for small stations, the annual reports show it and Content Credentials stay voluntary. The 2030 review is our formal check.

Panel questionThe Mainland already requires labels. Why does Hong Kong need its own rule?

Mainland rules do not bind Hong Kong licensees. Hong Kong has its own broadcasting law and regulator, so the gap is real. Our chapter fills it with the tools Hong Kong already has, and it fits the national direction.

Method

How we built the evidence

What we did

  • Read the CA's public report on the free TV renewal, the Consumer Council and EOC submissions, and the 13 October 2026 renewal decision.
  • Read the Broadcasting Ordinance sanctions sections and the CA's sanctions guide.
  • Ran a desk review of AI use and published AI policies at TVB, RTHK, HOY and ViuTV.
  • Compared rules in the EU, Korea, the Mainland and the UK.
  • Ran a peer stress test on 10 November and changed the enforcement and cost design as a result.

What we cannot prove yet

  • How often stations already label AI on air. Our desk review cannot see every broadcast.
  • Whether Hong Kong viewers understand AI labels. This is why we propose a 12-month audience survey.
  • The exact compliance cost for each station. We rely on case examples (France Télévisions, Fuji TV, Suspilne), not Hong Kong data.

Course grounding

Roberts and Crawford: make the hidden visible

Roberts shows that hidden work behind the screen is a design choice. Crawford shows that AI rests on extraction that users never see. Rule 1 and Rule 4 make the machine, and the vendor behind it, visible.

Wishart: human oversight and public guidelines

Wishart's chapter on AI argues that outlets should publish their AI guidelines and never publish AI content without human oversight. Rules 3 and 4 turn that newsroom ethic into a broadcast standard.

Week 9: who holds power, who depends on whom

TVB's partnership on ByteDance's Seedance model shows how a Hong Kong broadcaster can come to depend on one outside model. The annual report puts that dependence on the record.

Ladder of Verification and Flew et al.

"We use AI responsibly" is the lowest rung: the company says so. Logs, reports and spot checks move the claim up the ladder. Flew et al.'s "return of the state" explains why national broadcast codes are still a real lever.
